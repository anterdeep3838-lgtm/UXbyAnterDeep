Breaking down the challenge

Four W's and a How, before a single screen.

I broke the problem into five questions. Each answer became a rule for what KwicK had to do.

4W+HKWICK
W

Who has the problem?

  • Households where something just broke
  • Families without a "known" technician nearby
  • People new to a city, renting
W

What is the problem?

  • No clear idea of when help will arrive
  • No way to judge skill or fit
  • Prices set after the visit
W

Why does it matter?

  • Leaks and power faults get worse by the hour
  • One unfair bill breaks trust for good
W

Where does it break?

  • Explaining a technical fault on a call
  • Choosing between unknown names
  • The wait, with no status
H

How will KwicK solve it?

  • Adaptive questions, not a form
  • Profiles matched to the answers
  • Estimate, arrival and status in one place

    What I learned

    Four Things I’ll Carry Into My Next Project

    01

    A working screen isn't a correct one.

    Although every button worked correctly, “Best match” recommended a kitchen specialist for a bathroom repair, highlighting the need to test outcomes, not just functionality.

    02

    Write the rule, not the fix.

    One rule, "never fake live status", stopped a whole family of bugs before they existed. Every later change traced back to the brief.

    03

    Cut what repeats.

    A section that looks good but does nothing new still costs attention. Asking "what is this section for?" was the fastest design tool I had.

    04

    Honest beats impressive.

    Labelling reviews and estimates as samples made the prototype more believable, not less.

Key Design Decisions & Trade-offs

Four choices that shaped Worth.

Each one gave up something small to make recording, understanding and staying on budget easier.

Decision 01

Finly answers from your data

Chose
An assistant that answers only from the user's own transactions.
Instead of
A general chatbot or static money tips.
Trade-off
A narrower scope, but every answer is personal and checkable.

Decision 02

Analysis apart from Home

Chose
Home gives the overview; Analysis explains the month.
Instead of
Charts stacked on Home.
Trade-off
One extra tap for charts, and a much calmer Home.

Decision 03

Voice, with a review step

Chose
Speak one sentence, then check what Worth understood.
Instead of
Saving whatever was heard straight away.
Trade-off
One extra tap, and no silently wrong entries.

Decision 04

Alert before the limit

Chose
Alerts at 70, 80 or 90% of each budget, plus a safe daily pace.
Instead of
A warning only once a limit breaks.
Trade-off
More notifications, so users choose the threshold.
Worth14 · Iterations

UX Approach & Iterations

Three iterations, up close.

Where I started, what I noticed, what I changed, and why the new version works better.

Iteration 01 · Add transaction

  1. StartedOne long form with every field on one page.
  2. NoticedThe amount, the one field that matters, competed with eight others; Save sat below the fold.
  3. ChangedAmount first on a keypad, then details with smart defaults like Today and UPI.
  4. BecauseOne decision per step is faster and leads to fewer mistakes.

Iteration 02 · Money Left card

  1. StartedA big number, two tiles and an unlabelled lime bar.
  2. NoticedThe card didn't say which dates it covered or what the bar measured.
  3. ChangedA date chip, a “Spent 65%” legend, a comparison line and a hide button.
  4. BecauseA number is only trusted when its context is visible.

Iteration 03 · Budget

  1. StartedEdit and delete icons on every category row.
  2. NoticedChanges sat right next to everyday reading, and status was only a bar.
  3. ChangedAn overall gauge with On track, Approaching or Over, a daily pace, and edits behind “Edit limit”.
  4. BecauseReading should feel safe; changes should be deliberate.
WorthUX laws

UX Laws in Practice

Six principles, built into the screens.

Each law is shown where it lives in the Worth prototype.

Postel's law

What it is: Accept flexible input, but return a clear and consistent result.
In Worth: Voice accepts phrases like “about 2k”, then presents a structured result to review.

Fitts's law

What it is: Important actions should be large and easy to reach.
In Worth: Add is the dock's largest target and sits directly under the thumb.

Hick's law

What it is: Fewer, clearer choices reduce decision time.
In Worth: Eight distinct icon categories keep classification quick and scannable.

Tesler's law

What it is: Necessary complexity should be handled by the product where possible.
In Worth: The keypad calculates split bills so users do not need to work them out elsewhere.

Goal-gradient effect

What it is: Motivation increases as progress toward a goal becomes visible.
In Worth: Progress bars and a monthly saving amount make each goal feel achievable.

Peak-end rule

What it is: People remember the strongest moment and the ending of an experience.
In Worth: Every entry ends with a clear receipt that confirms exactly what was saved.

Worth18 · Results

18 · Results

What Worth delivers.

  1. Answers on demand

    Finly explains spending from the user's own transactions.

  2. Log with confidence

    Three calm steps, voice with review, and a receipt.

  3. See the pattern

    One answer first, then categories and fair comparisons.

  4. No budget surprises

    Alerts at 70, 80 or 90%, and goals turned into monthly amounts.

What I learned

Hierarchy beats features. The redesign added little; it reordered almost everything.

Trust needs a review step. Voice and AI feel safe when people can see and correct what the app understood.
